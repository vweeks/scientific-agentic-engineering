Charting a Course
for AI in Science

Rather than bolting AI onto research in a scattershot way, the scientific community needs a deliberative approach — one that keeps asking, at every stage, what AI can do versus what it should do.

Catherine Aiken · Steph Batalis · Greg Tananbaum CSET (Georgetown) & ORCA Vol. XLII · No. 3 · Spring 2026 read original ↗
01

The question under everything

AI is reshaping the whole scientific enterprise — for individual researchers, across fields, and for the system as a whole. The authors argue that the single thread running through every decision is a deceptively simple one.

can VS should

What AI is capable of doing is not the same as what it ought to do for science.

02

AI is both the subject and the tool

Writing from CSET, the authors see the shift through two lenses at once — and that double vantage is where the tensions surface.

Research subject

They study AI

LLMs like those behind ChatGPT, and scientific models like AlphaFold — plus the broader trends shaping how AI develops across the science-and-tech landscape.

Research tool

They build with AI

LLM-driven workflows clean and curate metadata for their Map of Science and translate, summarize, and classify documents — turning months of scripting into days. Yet that very leverage amplifies the value of human judgment: deciding what data to include, validating outputs, and documenting systems.

Adoption isn't driven by AI's power alone. A set of structural pressures is funneling researchers toward fast, high-volume tool use — sometimes regardless of its effect on rigor.

05

AI is altering every stage of research

Because AI touches the whole research process, the authors walk it stage by stage — pairing what AI can do with the tension each capability surfaces.

STAGE 01

Ideate & Design

AI can

Accelerate literature reviews, generate hypotheses, and design experiments — marketed as "co-scientists" and research partners.

The tension

Hallucinated citations and exaggerated hypotheses; validation failures; and a quieter shift in how scientists learn from prior work and form ideas.

STAGE 02

Conduct & Experiment

AI can

Run self-driving labs for chemical and biological experiments, and even act as "AI interviewers" with human subjects at unmatched scale.

The tension

Ethical conduct and oversight haven't caught up — and automation erodes the value of face-to-face and field engagement, for subjects and researchers alike.

STAGE 03

Interpret Results

AI can

Derive conclusions rapidly — protein-structure prediction (e.g. AlphaFold) being the canonical example of speed over lab drudgery.

The tension

Predictions can obscure real-world messiness and rarely explain their reasoning; they must be coupled with human scientists who contextualize and interpret.

STAGE 04

Vet & Communicate

AI can

Ease a straining peer-review system — citation checks, metadata validation, ethics screening, and paper-mill detection.

The tension

When publication is the career metric, the same tools get used to generate manuscripts — blurring who, or what, did the work.

06

Efficiency carries a hidden bill

Hurried, ad hoc adoption tilts science toward three lopsided trades — and risks entrenching the enterprise's existing flaws while widening the gap between the resourced and the rest.

Fig. 3 — Each trade favors the left when AI is adopted without deliberate guardrails.

  • Outsourced core work. Handing literature review, data collection, analysis, and writing to AI can cost researchers the familiarity that underpins real expertise.
  • Deepening inequity. Uneven access to tools and compute concentrates power among a small subset of well-resourced researchers and institutions.
  • Eroding trust. If claims can't be audited or interpreted — and AI quietly amplifies human bias — science's legitimacy as a shared enterprise weakens.
07

The remedy: deliberate "speed bumps"

The fix isn't to halt AI but to build sustained space to ask questions, reflect, and debate — paired with a stronger evidence base. The authors propose two reinforcing tracks.

Fig. 4 — Ongoing forums and a growing evidence base feed each other, rather than running as one-off events.

A scientist-led, distributed responsibility

No single entity owns this. The work is spread across an ecosystem already equipped to convene:

Convening bodiesNASEM, AAAS, and the American Academy of Arts & Sciences drive sustained, cross-disciplinary gatherings.
NonprofitsGroups like ORCA assemble focused coalitions around specific problems.
Field communitiesEfforts such as Responsible AI × Biodesign coordinate norms across disciplines.
ConferencesMetascience (ICSSI) and AI venues (AAAI, NeurIPS) can carve out reflective sessions.
Federal fundersAgencies like NSF can sponsor studies of researchers' AI use and build evidence.
The communityCoordinated, not centralized — and led by scientists themselves.
08

Science has done this before

Collective course-correction has precedent — moments when the community stepped in to shape a technology's trajectory rather than watch it unfold.

Fig. 5 — Precedents for coordinated, scientist-led deliberation cited by the authors.

The choice is no longer whether AI will reshape science and its bond with society — it's whether the scientific community plays an active, strategic role in how, or simply watches from the margins.
